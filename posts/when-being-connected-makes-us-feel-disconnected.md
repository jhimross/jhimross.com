I never consciously decided to spend so much of my life online.

It happened slowly.

A quick check of Facebook. A notification. A message. A few minutes of scrolling while waiting for something. Then another app. Another notification.

None of it felt serious.

Until I realized how often I was reaching for my phone **without even knowing why.**

That was probably the moment I started asking myself:

**Am I using technology, or is technology using me?**

## I Love Technology. That's the Problem.

I actually love technology.

I work with it. I learn through it. I use it to communicate, create, and connect with people. I don't want to live without it.

But I started noticing something uncomfortable.

Technology was no longer something I always chose to use. Sometimes, it had become my default response to boredom, silence, and waiting.

If I had nothing to do, I reached for my phone.

If I was waiting, I reached for my phone.

If I felt uncomfortable with silence, I reached for my phone.

I wasn't necessarily looking for anything.

**I was just checking.**

And that made me realize that maybe the problem wasn't technology itself.

**It was my relationship with it.**

## I Started Asking a Different Question

I used to ask:

> "Is this app useful?"

Almost everything is useful somehow.

Instead, I'm learning to ask:

> **"Does this actually support the life I want?"**

That's a much harder question.

Social media can help me connect with people. YouTube can help me learn. Messaging apps help me communicate. The internet gives me access to an incredible amount of knowledge.

But something can be useful and still take too much from me.

My attention.

My time.

My ability to focus.

Sometimes, even my presence with other people.

## I'm Learning to Be Okay With Missing Out

This might be the hardest lesson.

I don't need to see every post.

I don't need to know every trending topic.

I don't need to respond immediately to everything.

I don't need to be constantly available.

There will always be something happening online.

Another notification.

Another video.

Another conversation.

Another thing I could be looking at.

I can't keep up with all of it.

And maybe I shouldn't.

**Missing something online doesn't necessarily mean I'm missing out on life.**

## The Biggest Lesson: Don't Just Remove the Screen

I've also learned that simply using my phone less isn't enough.

If I remove the distractions but don't replace them with anything meaningful, I'll eventually go back.

So I'm trying to make room for other things.

Reading.

Walking as a form of exercise while feeding stray cats along the way. Somehow, those simple walks have become therapeutic for me. There's something calming about being outside, moving, and stopping for a few minutes to feed a cat. No screen needed. Just a quiet moment.

Coffee without constantly checking my phone.

Talking to people without looking at a screen.

Doing things that don't need to be posted, liked, or measured.

There's something refreshing about doing something simply because **I enjoy doing it.**

No notification.

No algorithm.

No audience.

Just me, doing something I care about.

## I'm Still Learning

I'm not writing this because I've mastered digital minimalism.

I haven't.

I still scroll longer than I should.

I still get distracted.

I still reach for my phone sometimes for no reason.

But now I notice it.

And I think that's where change begins.

I'm not trying to become a digital monk.

I don't want to abandon technology.

I just want technology to return to its proper place in my life:

**a tool, not a master.**

Maybe that's what digital minimalism really means to me.

Not necessarily using technology less, but becoming more intentional about **what deserves my attention.**

Because my attention is part of my life.

And I don't want to give my life away one notification at a time.
