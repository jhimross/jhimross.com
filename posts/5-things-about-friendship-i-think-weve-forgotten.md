Lately, I've been thinking about friendship and how it changes as we get older.

When we're younger, making friends seems so natural. You meet someone, share a few laughs, discover something in common, and before you know it, you're spending time together.

But as we grow older, things change. Work, responsibilities, and personal goals start taking up more of our time. Some friendships grow stronger, others slowly fade, and people who were once a big part of our lives become people we barely talk to anymore.

Sometimes, nobody does anything wrong. Life just happens.

I've also realized that knowing a lot of people isn't the same as having people who truly know you. We can keep up with each other through social media, exchange messages, and occasionally catch up. But how often do we have conversations where we can honestly talk about what's happening in our lives?

It makes me wonder if, in a world where staying connected is easier than ever, we've somehow forgotten how to be genuinely present for one another.

When I look at what the Bible says about relationships, I'm reminded that friendship isn't just something we fit into our schedules when we have extra time. It's part of how God created us to live.

Here are five things about friendship that I think we've forgotten.

## 1. Even in Paradise, We Were Never Meant to Be Alone

One of the things I find interesting about the creation story is that everything was good. There was no sin, suffering, or brokenness.

And yet, in Genesis 2:18, God said:

_&ldquo;It is not good that the man should be alone.&rdquo;_

Adam was in the Garden of Eden, living in a world untouched by sin. His relationship with God was unhindered, and yet God recognized that something was missing.

Adam needed human companionship.

I find that significant because we sometimes treat loneliness as a sign of spiritual weakness. We might think that if we trust God enough, pray regularly, and find our satisfaction in Him, we shouldn't need anyone else.

But that's not what this passage teaches.

Our relationship with God is foundational, but that doesn't mean human relationships are unnecessary. God Himself acknowledged our need for companionship.

We need people we can laugh with, learn from, pray with, and turn to when life gets difficult.

There's something comforting about knowing that even in a perfect garden, companionship mattered.

Maybe our desire for friendship isn't a weakness to overcome. Maybe it's a reminder that God created us for relationships.

## 2. Friendship Is More Than a Nice Extra

It's easy to treat friendship as something we'll prioritize when life gets less busy.

We'll catch up after finishing a project. We'll schedule coffee when work settles down. We'll reconnect when we finally have some free time.

The problem is that life rarely settles down for long.

Before we realize it, months have passed since we last had a meaningful conversation with someone we care about.

But friendship isn't just about having someone to hang out with. Meaningful social connections also contribute to our emotional and physical well-being, while persistent loneliness and social isolation are associated with health risks.

Proverbs 18:1 warns:

_&ldquo;Whoever isolates himself seeks his own desire; he breaks out against all sound judgment.&rdquo;_

Of course, enjoying solitude isn't wrong. Jesus Himself withdrew from crowds to pray and spend time with the Father. There's a difference between choosing time alone and cutting ourselves off from meaningful relationships.

Sometimes, we become so accustomed to handling everything ourselves that asking for help feels uncomfortable. We convince ourselves that we don't need anyone until life becomes difficult.

Maybe we're overwhelmed at work, grieving a loss, or struggling with something we don't know how to explain.

During those moments, a friend who listens, checks in, or prays with us can make a real difference.

We weren't designed to carry everything alone. Friendship deserves a place in our lives, even when we're busy.

## 3. We Have Plenty of Acquaintances, but Do We Have Real Friends?

We can know hundreds of people without feeling truly known by any of them.

Think about it. How many people know what you're worried about lately? What you've been praying for? What you're struggling with but haven't told anyone?

There's nothing wrong with casual friendships. Not every relationship needs to become deeply personal. But there's a difference between being surrounded by people and having someone you can trust with the parts of yourself you don't show everyone.

I sometimes think of friendship as the difference between snorkeling and diving deep into the ocean.

Snorkeling lets you enjoy the surface. You talk about work, hobbies, and everyday life. Those conversations matter, too.

But deeper friendship happens when you can say, "Honestly, I'm not doing very well," without feeling the need to pretend everything is fine.

It's being able to admit a mistake, share a fear, or ask someone to pray for you when your faith feels weak.

The challenge is that deeper friendships require time and attention. Technology makes it easy to keep up with people's lives, but seeing someone's posts isn't the same as knowing how they're really doing.

Sometimes, we need to put our phones away and have an uninterrupted conversation. We need to ask better questions and genuinely listen to the answers.

We don't need hundreds of close friends. But we do need relationships where honesty, trust, and mutual care can grow.

The question isn't simply how many people we know. It's whether we're willing to let ourselves be known, too.

## 4. Friendship Isn't a Lesser Kind of Love

As Christians, we often talk about _agape_&mdash;selfless, sacrificial love. And rightly so. We're called to love others even when it's difficult or inconvenient.

But I wonder if we sometimes overlook friendship because we see it as less important than other expressions of love.

Jesus had something remarkable to say about it.

In John 15:13, He said:

_&ldquo;Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends.&rdquo;_

And in John 15:15, He told His disciples:

_&ldquo;No longer do I call you servants . . . but I have called you friends.&rdquo;_

Jesus didn't treat friendship as insignificant. He shared life with His disciples, taught them, corrected them, and continued to love them despite their weaknesses.

Ultimately, He laid down His life for them.

That gives us a much deeper picture of friendship than simply having someone to spend time with when things are going well.

Our friendships won't always be perfect. We'll misunderstand each other, make mistakes, and sometimes cause hurt. But friendship gives us opportunities to practice patience, humility, forgiveness, and faithfulness.

Sometimes, loving a friend means offering advice. Other times, it means listening without judgment or staying beside them when there's nothing we can do to fix their situation.

We may not have all the answers, but we can remind them they're not alone.

Friendship is one of the ways love becomes tangible in our everyday lives.

## 5. Don't Wait Until It's Too Late to Reach Out

One thing that puts life into perspective is realizing that we don't have unlimited time with the people we love.

In her book _The Top Five Regrets of the Dying_, Bronnie Ware describes a regret shared by some of the people she cared for: wishing they had stayed in touch with their friends.

It's easy to understand how that happens.

You get busy. Your friend gets busy. You promise to catch up soon, but soon becomes next month, and next month becomes next year.

Eventually, reaching out feels awkward because so much time has passed.

But not every friendship needs a grand reunion to begin again. Sometimes, all it takes is a simple message.

There's a moment in Paul's final letter to Timothy that I find particularly meaningful. While writing from prison and facing the end of his life, Paul asked Timothy to come to him.

In 2 Timothy 4:9, he wrote:

_&ldquo;Do your best to come to me soon.&rdquo;_

And in verse 21:

_&ldquo;Do your best to come before winter.&rdquo;_

Paul had devoted his life to serving Christ, yet he still wanted the company of someone he cared about.

His faith didn't eliminate his need for companionship.

I think that's an important reminder. We can be devoted to God, committed to our work, and focused on our responsibilities while still needing the people who walk alongside us.

We don't know how much time we have with the people in our lives. So if there's a friend you haven't spoken to in a while, don't overthink it.

Send a message. Make a call. Ask them to grab coffee.

You don't need a special occasion to remind someone that they matter to you.

## A Few Thoughts Before You Go

The older I get, the more I think we need to be intentional about the relationships that matter to us.

Friendships don't always disappear because of conflict. Sometimes, they fade simply because we stop making room for them.

And maintaining them doesn't have to be complicated.

Sometimes, it's sharing a cup of coffee and talking about life. Sometimes, it's going for a walk, praying together, or checking in after a difficult week.

As someone whose faith is in Jesus, I'm reminded that God created us not only to love Him but also to love the people around us. We don't have to pretend we have everything figured out, and we don't have to walk through life alone.

So here's something to think about:

**If you could spend an afternoon with three friends, simply talking about life, who would you choose?**

When was the last time you spoke to them?

Maybe there's someone you've been meaning to reconnect with. Perhaps this is a good time to reach out.

Life is already full of things demanding our attention. Let's not become so busy living that we forget the people who make life meaningful.

Because friendship isn't just about having people around us. It's about sharing life's joys and struggles with people we're grateful to have beside us.

And that's worth making time for.
